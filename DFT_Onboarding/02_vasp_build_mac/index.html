02 — Building VASP Week 2 — working session

Compiling VASP from source against a conda-only toolchain on an Apple Silicon Mac.

This module is deliberately not a recipe. There is no list of commands here to copy, and that is on purpose. We will do this together, in the Week 2 meeting, with Elif in the room and Claude Code driving the build interactively.

Compiling scientific software on a machine nobody has built it on before is genuinely fiddly, and it is not a solved problem you can look up. Watching how the problem gets diagnosed is the entire point of the session. A recipe would rob you of that, and it would also be a lie — any recipe written in advance would be guessing at your specific machine.
Goal. A working vasp_std binary that runs in parallel on your laptop, built entirely from conda-forge packages. Plus notes good enough that the next student has a real starting point.
This has been done, successfully, on this toolchain. A previous student built vasp_std, vasp_gam and vasp_ncl from a pure conda-forge toolchain on Apple Silicon and wrote down exactly what it took: BUILD_NOTES_2026-08_M5Pro.md.

Read it after your first failure, not before. It is a record of one machine at one moment, not a recipe — package versions move and your errors will differ. But when the first compile dies and it feels like a dead end, it is worth knowing that the path exists and what the far side looked like.

1. Why this is harder than installing a normal package

You have spent module 01 installing things with conda install. VASP does not work that way, for three reasons:

  • It is licensed, closed source. You get a tarball of Fortran source code, not a package. It is not on conda-forge, not on PyPI, and never will be. Elif holds the group license and it covers installation on your laptop.
  • You must compile it yourself. That means turning ~200,000 lines of Fortran into a binary, correctly linked against an MPI implementation, a linear algebra library, and an FFT library.
  • Apple Silicon is not the common case. Most VASP installations in the world are on Intel/Linux clusters using Intel's compilers and MKL. Almost every build guide you find online assumes that. We are on arm64 macOS with GNU compilers from conda-forge, which is a legitimate but much less trodden path.
Why not just use the cluster and skip this? Because a calculation you can start in five seconds and restart after a mistake is a calculation you will actually learn from. Queue waits break the tight feedback loop that makes the first two months productive. You will move to the cluster in Week 12, once you know what you are doing and the calculations are genuinely too big — not before.

2. Before the meeting

Have all of this ready. If any piece is missing we lose the session.

Pre-meeting checklist

  • Module 01 complete — check_setup.py passes every required check.
  • The VASP source tarball from Elif, downloaded and unpacked somewhere sensible (e.g. ~/software/vasp.6.x.x/). Know the exact path.
  • The POTCAR library in place from module 01.
  • conda activate defects works and the environment has the compiler and library packages in it.
  • VS Code open, with the Claude Code extension working.
  • At least 10 GB free disk space and your laptop plugged in. Compiling is CPU-heavy and will drain a battery fast.

What the build needs, conceptually

You do not need to know how to configure these — but you should know what the pieces are, so the conversation in the meeting means something:

PieceWhat it doesconda-forge package
Fortran compiler Turns the source into machine code. gfortran
MPI Lets VASP split work across your CPU cores and communicate between them. openmpi
BLAS / LAPACK Dense linear algebra — matrix multiplication, eigenvalue problems. Most of VASP's time is spent here. openblas
ScaLAPACK The distributed-memory version of LAPACK, for problems split across MPI ranks. scalapack
FFTW Fast Fourier transforms — the other half of where the time goes, moving between real and reciprocal space. fftw
Why these two libraries in particular? Plane-wave DFT is, computationally, mostly two operations repeated an enormous number of times: FFTs to move wavefunctions between real space and reciprocal space, and dense linear algebra to orthogonalize and diagonalize. That is why the quality of the BLAS and FFT libraries matters so much more than the quality of the compiler, and why "which BLAS did you link against?" is a reasonable question to ask about someone's benchmark.

3. How the session will go

Roughly this shape. Expect detours.

  1. Confirm the toolchain. Check that gfortran, mpifort, and mpirun all resolve to binaries inside ~/miniforge3/envs/defects/ and not somewhere else. which is your friend.
  2. Look at what VASP provides. The source tree has an arch/ directory full of makefile.include templates for different compiler and platform combinations. One of the GNU/OpenMPI ones is the starting point. None of them will be exactly right.
  3. Adapt it. This is the real work: pointing the library and include paths at $CONDA_PREFIX instead of wherever the template expects, and getting the preprocessor flags right. This is where you bring Claude in — give it the template, the output of conda list, and your CONDA_PREFIX, and work through it.
  4. Compile. make std, or similar. It will take a while and it will probably fail the first time. Failure here is the normal case, not a sign something is wrong with you or your machine.
  5. Read the error and iterate. The bulk of the session. See the failure categories below.
  6. Test. Run something small and check the answer is physically sensible, not just that the binary launched.

Working with Claude on this

Some things that make the difference between Claude being useful and being a time sink:

  • Give it context up front, not one error at a time: the platform (uname -m), the compiler versions, the relevant conda list output, $CONDA_PREFIX, and the makefile.include you are starting from.
  • Paste the whole error, not the last line. Fortran link errors are long and the useful part is often in the middle.
  • Ask it to explain the fix, not just make it. "Why did that flag matter?" is the question that makes this session worth ninety minutes instead of being a black box.
  • Keep a log. Every change you make to makefile.include, and why. This becomes the deliverable.

4. Failure categories you are likely to meet

Named here so you recognize them rather than being defeated by them. No solutions — the solutions depend on your machine, and finding them is the exercise.

SymptomWhat it usually means
ld: library not found for -lxxx The linker cannot find a library. Usually a path pointing outside the conda environment, or a package that is not actually installed.
Symbol not found / undefined reference at link time Something compiled, but the function it calls does not exist in the libraries provided. Often a BLAS/ScaLAPACK naming or version mismatch.
Compiles fine, crashes immediately on run Frequently an integer-size mismatch (32-bit vs 64-bit array indexing) between VASP and the BLAS library. A classic, and confusing because the build looked clean.
mpirun runs N independent copies instead of one N-core job VASP was compiled without MPI support, or against a different MPI than the mpirun on your PATH. Diagnostic: the output will show N identical calculations rather than one distributed one.
Preprocessor / #define errors The CPP_OPTIONS line in makefile.include needs adjusting for this platform.
Runs, but gives an obviously wrong energy The most dangerous category, because nothing announces itself. This is why step 6 tests against a known answer.
A clean build is not a correct build. A binary that compiles, links, and runs can still be numerically wrong — most often through a silently mismatched math library. This is exactly why the validation step below compares against a known answer rather than just checking that a number came out.

5. What success looks like

Three tests, in order. Do not skip the third.

Test 1 — the binary exists and is executable

ls -lh /path/to/vasp.6.x.x/bin/vasp_std
file /path/to/vasp.6.x.x/bin/vasp_std     # should say arm64, not x86_64

Test 2 — it actually runs in parallel

Go to module 05's directory, which has a complete silicon input set, and run:

conda activate defects
mpirun -np 4 /path/to/vasp_std | tail -20

Then check the top of the OUTCAR:

grep -i "running on" OUTCAR

It should report 4 cores in one job. If it says 1 core, or if you see four separate calculations starting, MPI is not working — that is failure category 4 above.

Test 3 — the answer is right

This is the test that matters. Run the silicon calculation and check the total energy:

grep "free  energy   TOTEN" OUTCAR | tail -1

For the 8-atom silicon cell in module 05 with those inputs, you should get roughly −43 eV (about −5.4 eV per atom).

Do not expect an exact match to any number written here — it depends on the POTCAR version and VASP version. But you should be within a fraction of an eV per atom. If you get −5.4 eV/atom, the build is sound. If you get something wildly different, or NaN, the build is broken regardless of how cleanly it compiled.

Make it easy to call

Once it works, add a shortcut to ~/.zshrc so you are not typing the full path forever:

export VASP_STD="$HOME/software/vasp.6.x.x/bin/vasp_std"
alias vasp4="mpirun -np 4 $VASP_STD"
alias vasp8="mpirun -np 8 $VASP_STD"

6. Deliverable

Two things, both committed to your repository:

  1. Your working makefile.include, in a vasp_build/ directory. This is the single most valuable artifact of the session.
  2. A build log in Markdown — BUILD_NOTES.md — recording:
    • Machine and OS version, and the output of uname -m
    • Exact conda package versions of the toolchain (conda list | grep -E "gfortran|openmpi|openblas|fftw|scalapack")
    • Which arch/ template you started from
    • Every change you made, and why
    • Errors you hit and what fixed them
    • The validation energy from test 3
Write the notes during the session, not after. You will not remember the third error by the time you fix the sixth. This document is what turns a two-hour struggle into fifteen minutes for the next person.

That is not hypothetical: the build record linked at the top of this page was written exactly this way, by the student before you. Yours joins it.

7. After the build

Go straight to module 03 and learn what the four input files actually are. You will have just run a calculation without understanding its inputs, which is fine as a build test and not fine as a way of working.

Before moving on

  • vasp_std exists, is arm64, and is executable.
  • It runs on multiple cores as one job, confirmed in the OUTCAR.
  • The silicon test energy is about −5.4 eV/atom.
  • makefile.include and BUILD_NOTES.md are committed.
  • I have an alias or environment variable so I do not type the full path.
  • I could explain to someone else what MPI, BLAS, and FFTW each do here.
