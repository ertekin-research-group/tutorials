07 — HPC and SLURM Week 12

Moving the workflow to the group cluster partition: SSH, the filesystem, Lmod modules, SLURM, and file transfer.

Goal. Run the same calculations you have been running locally on the cluster, at supercell sizes your laptop cannot reach, and verify that your laptop-scale conclusions survive.
Why this comes so late. You will have done an entire native-defect study on your laptop before opening this module. That is deliberate. Queue waits break the tight edit-run-look loop that makes learning fast, and a cluster adds a whole second category of problems — schedulers, modules, filesystems, disk quotas — on top of the physics. By Week 12 the physics is familiar, so the new problems are isolated and therefore tractable.

1. Logging in

ssh yournetid@cluster.address.edu
exit                                  # disconnect

Set up a key so you are not typing a password every time. You already did this for GitHub in module 01, so you may be able to reuse the key:

ssh-copy-id yournetid@cluster.address.edu
Save yourself typing with an entry in ~/.ssh/config on your laptop: 
Host ccc
    HostName cluster.address.edu
    User yournetid
    ServerAliveInterval 60
Then it is just ssh ccc, and scp file ccc:~/ works too. ServerAliveInterval stops idle connections from being dropped.

2. The cluster filesystem

PathPurposePersistent?
/u/yournetid (home) Code, configs, scripts, small results. Yes — backed up. Usually a modest quota.
/scratch/yournetid Large intermediate files: WAVECAR, CHGCAR during a run. No — purged periodically without warning.
/sw, /apps System software. Read-only.
Never put anything you cannot regenerate in /scratch. Not your VASP binary, not your POTCAR library, not your scripts, not your only copy of a result. Scratch is for transient run output that you copy off afterwards. Purges are automatic and there is no recovery.

Corollary: the authoritative copy of your work lives in Git, which means it lives in three places at once — your laptop, GitHub, and the cluster. That is the point.

Watch your quota

du -sh ~                    # how much am I using?
df -h ~                     # how much is there?
du -h -d 1 . | sort -h      # what in this directory is big?
VASP output is enormous at scale. A 300-atom WAVECAR can be several GB, and a defect study has dozens of calculations. Set LWAVE = .FALSE. and LCHARG = .FALSE. unless you specifically need them, and clean up as you go rather than discovering you are over quota with thirty jobs queued.

3. Lmod modules

Cluster software is loaded on demand rather than being on PATH by default:

module avail                     # everything available
module avail intel               # filter
module load intel/mpi/2021.14    # load one
module list                      # what is loaded now
module purge                     # unload everything
module spider vasp               # search across all versions
module avail vasp returns nothing, and that is expected. VASP is licensed and is not distributed as a cluster module — you compile your own, exactly as you did on your laptop in module 02. The module load intel/... lines in the submit script only pull in the MPI and MKL runtime libraries that the cluster build was linked against.

4. How the cluster differs from your laptop

Read this section carefully. Most cluster problems come from assuming the two environments are the same.

LaptopCluster
Toolchainconda-forge: gfortran, OpenMPI, OpenBLAS Intel compilers, Intel MPI, MKL — loaded via module
How you run./run_local.sh, immediately sbatch vasp_submit, then wait in a queue
condaMust be activated Must not be activated for VASP jobs
Cores~816+ per node, many nodes
FeedbackSecondsMinutes to hours of queue time
Do not conda activate in a SLURM job script. This is the most common laptop-to-cluster mistake. Your conda environment carries its own OpenMPI, which will shadow the Intel MPI the cluster binary was built against. The job then fails in a genuinely confusing way — often by launching N independent single-core calculations instead of one N-core job. If you need Python for post-processing on the cluster, do that in a separate step, not inside the VASP job.

Two builds, two binaries

You will have two VASP binaries: the conda-linked one on your laptop and an Intel-linked one on the cluster. They should give the same answers to within numerical noise. Verify this rather than assuming it — rerun one laptop calculation on the cluster and compare. A discrepancy beyond a meV or so per atom means one of the builds has a problem, and you want to discover that on a calculation whose answer you already know.

5. SLURM: submitting and managing jobs

CommandPurpose
sbatch vasp_submitSubmit a job. Prints the job ID.
squeue -u $USERYour jobs. The command you will run most.
scancel 123456Cancel one job.
sacct -j 123456After the fact: exit status, runtime, memory used.
sinfo -p eertekin-intelIs the partition busy?
scontrol show job 123456Everything about one job.

Job states in squeue

STMeaning
PDPending — waiting for resources.
RRunning.
CGCompleting (cleanup).
CDCompleted. Note: this means SLURM is happy, not that VASP converged.
FFailed.
TOTimed out — hit the --time limit.
CD does not mean your calculation is good. It means the script exited without error. VASP exits cleanly after hitting NELM without converging, and after hitting NSW with atoms still moving. This is why the vasp_submit script here prints an explicit convergence check at the end — the same habit as run_local.sh, carried over.

The submit script

vasp_submit in this directory is the group template. Read it — the comments explain each block. Per job, you edit two things:

  • --job-name — make it descriptive. GaN_VN_q1_216at tells you what it is in squeue; test4 does not.
  • --time — your best estimate plus margin. Too short and you get TO and lose the work; wildly too long and you wait longer in the queue.
Estimating --time. You have laptop timings for smaller cells. VASP scales roughly as the cube of the number of electrons, so tripling the cell size is a factor of ~27 in cost, offset by the extra cores. Estimate, then check sacct -j <id> on your first few jobs to calibrate. After a week you will guess well.

6. Moving files

# laptop -> cluster
scp POSCAR ccc:/u/yournetid/gan/defects/

# cluster -> laptop (run on the laptop)
scp ccc:/u/yournetid/gan/bands.png .

# whole directory, and smarter: only sends what changed, resumable
rsync -avP ~/local/gan/ ccc:/u/yournetid/gan/
rsync -avP ccc:/u/yournetid/gan/ ~/local/gan/
Prefer rsync over scp. It only transfers differences, shows progress, and resumes after an interruption. The trailing slashes matter: src/ copies the contents of src, while src copies the directory itself. Test with --dry-run first when you are not sure.

The better answer: use Git

For inputs, scripts, and small results, do not transfer files at all — git push from your laptop and git pull on the cluster. Your .gitignore from module 03 already excludes the large binaries, so this moves exactly what should move. Reserve rsync for bulk output that does not belong in version control.

Archiving

tar -czvf gan_defects.tar.gz gan_defects/    # create
tar -tzvf gan_defects.tar.gz                 # inspect without extracting
tar -xzvf gan_defects.tar.gz                 # extract

7. Managing many jobs

A native-defect study is dozens of calculations. Directory conventions matter far more than you expect — this is the difference between a study you can write up and one you have to redo.

Encode everything in the path. A directory name should identify a calculation uniquely without opening anything: 
gan/
  bulk/
    encut_conv/
    relax/
  defects/
    v_N/
      q_+1/  q_0/  q_-1/
    v_Ga/
      q_0/  q_-1/  q_-2/  q_-3/
    supercell_scaling/
      v_N_q0_032at/  v_N_q0_072at/  v_N_q0_216at/
By Week 13 you will have hundreds of these. doped imposes its own convention, which is one of the reasons to use it — but you should understand what a good convention looks like before a tool chooses one for you.

Useful one-liners

# energies from every finished run
grep "free  energy   TOTEN" */OUTCAR | tail -1

# which runs did NOT converge?
for d in */; do
    grep -q "reached required accuracy" "$d/OUTCAR" 2>/dev/null \
        || echo "NOT CONVERGED: $d"
done

# how many of my jobs are still queued vs running?
squeue -u $USER -h -t PD | wc -l
squeue -u $USER -h -t R  | wc -l
Check convergence across the whole set before analyzing any of it. With one calculation you notice a failure. With sixty, a single unconverged run hides silently and quietly poisons a formation energy diagram. Run the loop above every time, before you plot anything.

8. Survival tips

ActionHow
Watch a running jobtail -f OSZICAR
Session that survives a dropped connectiontmux, reattach with tmux attach
Reverse-search your command historyCtrl+R, then type a fragment
Repeat the last command!!
Stop a commandCtrl+C
What is using CPU on this nodetop — q to quit
Learn tmux on day one. Start a session on the login node before you begin monitoring anything. If your SSH connection drops — and it will, on hotel wifi or when your laptop sleeps — the session keeps running and you reattach where you left off.

Cluster etiquette

  • Never run a calculation on the login node. It is shared by everyone. Small Python post-processing is fine; VASP is not.
  • Do not request more resources than you can use. Asking for 4 nodes for a job that scales to 1 wastes the partition and makes your own queue wait longer.
  • Test small before submitting many. One job to verify the script works, then the batch. Submitting sixty broken jobs is a bad afternoon for you and for everyone else waiting.
  • Clean up. Delete WAVECARs you do not need. The partition is shared storage.

9. Deliverable

  1. A working vasp_submit in your repository, adapted to your paths.
  2. A cross-validation: one calculation run both on your laptop and on the cluster, with the energies compared. State the agreement.
  3. Large-supercell results for at least two defects, with a written comparison against your laptop-scale numbers. Does the corrected formation energy still agree? If not, that is an important finding — not a failure.
  4. A note on your directory convention, and the convergence-checking loop you used across the whole set.

Checklist

  • I can log in with a key and without typing a password.
  • I know which directories persist and which get purged.
  • I understand why conda activate must not appear in a job script.
  • I have verified the cluster and laptop builds agree on a known calculation.
  • I check convergence across every run in a set before analyzing any of it.
  • My directory names identify calculations without opening them.
  • I use tmux so a dropped connection does not lose my session.
