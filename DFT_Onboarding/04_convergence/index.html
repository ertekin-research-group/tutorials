04 — Convergence Testing Week 4

Establishing, and justifying, the numerical parameters that every calculation for the rest of the semester will use.

Goal. Determine converged values of ENCUT and the k-point mesh for silicon, with plots as evidence, and understand why this has to happen before you relax anything.

This is the most important module in the series. Everything after it is technique; this is the habit.
The rule. Never report a number you have not converged, and always be able to say how you know it is converged. Not "I used 520 eV because a paper did" — but "I used 450 eV because at 450 the energy per atom is within 1 meV of the value at 600, and here is the plot."

This is the difference between running software and doing computational science. A DFT calculation always produces a number. Whether that number means anything is entirely determined by work like this module.

1. What you are converging, and against what

Recall the distinction from module 03: POSCAR and POTCAR describe the physical system; INCAR and KPOINTS describe the numerical approximation. Two numerical parameters dominate the accuracy of a plane-wave DFT calculation:

ParameterWhat it controlsCost scaling
ENCUT How many plane waves are used to represent each wavefunction. Higher = a more complete basis set. Roughly as ENCUT3/2.
k-mesh How finely the Brillouin zone is sampled when integrating over reciprocal space. Roughly linear in the number of irreducible k-points.
"Converged" always means "converged with respect to something, to within some tolerance." It is never an absolute property of a calculation. A cutoff converged to 1 meV/atom for total energy may be badly unconverged for a stress tensor or a phonon frequency. When you write down a converged value, write down the quantity and the tolerance too.

The tolerance we will use

1 meV/atom for total energy. That is a reasonable default for the work we do: defect formation energies are typically 0.5–5 eV, so 1 meV/atom of numerical noise is comfortably below anything we care about physically.

The reference problem

You cannot compare against the exact answer, because you do not have it. So you compare against the most converged calculation you can afford, and watch the differences shrink. This works, but has a trap:

If only your largest value looks "converged", nothing is converged. Your reference deviates from itself by exactly zero, so it always looks perfect. The signal you want is a plateau: several consecutive values agreeing with each other. If the energy is still drifting in one direction at the top of your range, extend the range. The scripts here check for this and will tell you.

3. Part A — the ENCUT sweep

First, find the floor. The POTCAR recommends a minimum:

grep ENMAX POTCAR

For silicon that is about 245 eV. That is a floor, not a converged value — a common misreading. Now sweep well past it:

python sweep.py encut

This runs eight static calculations at ENCUT = 250 to 600 eV, with the k-mesh fixed at a generous 9×9×9 so that k-point sampling is not what limits the result. Each run takes a few seconds to a minute.

Then plot:

python plot_convergence.py encut
Look at the two panels and notice how different they are. The left panel — absolute energy per atom — looks essentially flat, because the total energy is around −5.4 eV/atom and you are chasing changes of a few thousandths of that. The right panel plots the deviation on a log scale, and that is where convergence is actually visible. If you take one plotting lesson from this module: to see convergence, plot the difference, on a log axis.

What to conclude

  • The smallest ENCUT within 1 meV/atom of the reference.
  • How much more the converged run costs than the cheapest one.
  • Whether the curve has genuinely plateaued, or is still sloping at 600 eV. If it is still sloping, rerun with --values 500 600 700 800.
Use the smallest converged value, not the largest one you tested. Over-converging is not "being careful" — it is spending two to three times the compute for no additional accuracy. You will run thousands of these calculations this semester, many of them on 96-atom defect supercells. A factor of two matters.

4. Part B — the k-mesh sweep

Now hold ENCUT at your converged value and sweep the mesh:

python sweep.py kpoints --encut 450        # use YOUR value from Part A
python plot_convergence.py kpoints
Why sweep them one at a time rather than together? In principle the two parameters are coupled and you should converge them jointly on a 2D grid. In practice they are close enough to independent that sequential convergence works, and it costs eight calculations instead of sixty-four. The safeguard is to fix the other parameter at a generous value while you sweep — which is why Part A used a 9×9×9 mesh. If you suspect coupling, spot-check: rerun one point from Part A at your final k-mesh and confirm the answer has not moved.
k-point convergence depends on cell size, and inversely. A large real-space cell has a small Brillouin zone, which needs fewer k-points to sample. Your silicon result here will not transfer to a 96-atom GaN supercell — that will need a much sparser mesh, and you will re-converge for it. What transfers is the method, not the number.

A useful way to report k-point density

Because the right mesh depends on the cell, "9×9×9" is not portable between systems. What is portable is the product of the mesh subdivision and the lattice vector length — often quoted as k-point spacing in Å−1, or as "n × a ≈ 30 Å". Compute this for your converged silicon mesh; it gives you a starting guess for GaN in Week 6 instead of starting from scratch.

5. Part C — differences converge faster than absolutes

This is the most important conceptual point in the module, and it is what makes defect calculations tractable at all.

Everything we care about physically is an energy difference: a formation energy, a defect formation energy, a reaction energy, a barrier. Absolute total energies are never reported and are not physically meaningful on their own. And it turns out differences converge much faster than the absolute energies they are built from, because the two calculations share most of the same convergence error, which cancels.

The experiment

Take two silicon cells at slightly different volumes — say a 1% compression — and compute the energy difference between them at each ENCUT in your Part A sweep. Then compare how fast that difference converges against how fast either absolute energy did.

# build a compressed POSCAR
python -c "
from pymatgen.core import Structure
s = Structure.from_file('POSCAR')
s.scale_lattice(s.volume * 0.99)
s.to(filename='POSCAR_compressed')
print('wrote POSCAR_compressed, volume', s.volume)
"

Then rerun the ENCUT sweep on the compressed cell (copy this directory, or swap the POSCAR), and plot the difference. You should find the difference converged at a noticeably lower cutoff than either absolute energy.

Why this matters enormously for us. A defect formation energy is the difference between a defect supercell and a perfect supercell of the same size. The basis-set error in each is large but nearly identical, so it largely cancels in the difference. This is why we can compute meaningful defect energies at cutoffs that would be far too low for absolute energies — and it is also why the two calculations in a difference must use exactly the same ENCUT, k-mesh, and POTCARs. Break that and you lose the cancellation, which is the entire reason the number is trustworthy.
Consequence, worth internalizing now. Never compare energies from calculations with different ENCUT, different k-meshes, or different POTCAR variants. The comparison is meaningless — not slightly worse, meaningless. This will be a live hazard from Week 8 onward, when you start subtracting supercell energies from each other.

6. Part D — Pulay stress: why convergence precedes relaxation

Everything so far used a fixed structure. That was deliberate. Now you get to see what happens if you relax a cell with an unconverged cutoff:

python pulay_demo.py --low 250 --high 550

This relaxes the same silicon cell twice (ISIF = 3, so both ions and cell are free), changing only ENCUT, and compares the resulting lattice constants.

What you will see

The two runs give measurably different lattice constants. Both relaxed successfully. Neither reported an error.

Why. The stress tensor VASP computes is the derivative of the energy with respect to strain at fixed basis set. But the plane-wave basis is defined by an energy cutoff in reciprocal space — so when the cell changes volume, the set of plane waves fitting under that cutoff changes too. The computed stress therefore misses a term: the derivative with respect to the changing basis. That missing term is Pulay stress.

It shrinks as ENCUT grows, and it is essentially absent for fixed-cell calculations, where the basis never changes. Hence the ordering: converge on a fixed structure, then relax.
This is the dangerous class of error. Not a crash, not a warning — a confident, converged, wrong answer. The low-ENCUT relaxation produces a lattice constant with a systematic bias and no indication anything is amiss. You cannot detect this from one calculation. The only defense is to have converged your parameters beforehand, which is why this module exists and why it is number 04 rather than number 06.

Two errors, distinguished

Your well-converged PBE lattice constant will still not match experiment exactly — it will land slightly above 5.431 Å. That residual is physics: a known systematic tendency of PBE, which no amount of convergence will remove.

Learning to separate "my numerics are not converged" from "this functional has a known systematic error" is one of the genuinely useful skills in this field. The first is your fault and is fixable. The second is a property of the method, and the right response is to know its size and direction. You will meet this distinction again immediately, in module 05 with the lattice constant and in module 06 with the band gap.

  2. Both convergence figures.
  3. The Part C comparison showing that a difference converges faster than an absolute energy.
  4. The Part D output, with the two lattice constants.
  5. A written statement of your converged parameters, of roughly this form:
    For silicon in the 8-atom conventional cell, total energy is converged to within 1 meV/atom at ENCUT = ___ eV (reference: ___ eV) and a ___×___×___ Gamma-centered mesh (reference: ___). Evidence: encut_convergence.png, kpoints_convergence.png. Energy differences between cells of the same size converge by ___ eV. Cell relaxation at 250 eV gives a lattice constant ___ Å larger than at 550 eV, consistent with Pulay stress.

That statement goes into the methods section of your semester report. Every calculation you report from here on cites it.

8. Pitfalls

PitfallWhy it is wrong
Taking ENCUT from a paper on a different material Converged ENCUT depends on the hardest POTCAR in your system. Oxygen and nitrogen need much more than silicon.
Treating ENMAX as the converged value It is a floor. Converged values are typically 1.3–2× higher.
Converging on a relaxed structure Circular: the relaxation depended on the parameters you are converging. Always converge at fixed geometry.
Assuming silicon's k-mesh transfers to GaN Different cell size, different lattice constants, different Brillouin zone. Re-converge.
Declaring convergence from two points You need a plateau. Two points cannot show one.
Adopting the largest tested value because "only it converged" Then nothing converged. Extend the range.
Comparing energies across different ENCUT Destroys the error cancellation that makes differences trustworthy.
Converging total energy and assuming everything else is converged Stresses, forces, and band gaps converge at different rates — usually slower.
