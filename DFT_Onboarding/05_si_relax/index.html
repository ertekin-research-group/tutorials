05 — Relaxing Silicon Week 5

Your first real prediction: the equilibrium lattice constant of silicon, computed from scratch and compared to experiment.

1. What relaxation means

Up to now every calculation has been static: fixed atoms, fixed cell, one self-consistent solution of the electronic problem. Relaxation adds an outer loop. VASP computes the forces on each atom and the stress on the cell, moves things downhill, and solves the electronic problem again. It repeats until the forces fall below your threshold.

So there are two nested convergence criteria, and they are easy to confuse:

LoopCriterionTagTypical value
Inner (electronic)Energy change between SCF steps EDIFF1E-6 eV
Outer (ionic)Largest force on any atom EDIFFG-0.01 eV/Å
The inner criterion must be tighter than the outer one. Forces are derivatives of the energy, so noise in the energy becomes larger noise in the force. If EDIFF is loose, the forces are garbage and the relaxation wanders. The rule of thumb is EDIFF at least two orders of magnitude tighter than the energy scale implied by your force criterion.

2. The INCAR, tag by tag

The relaxation-specific tags in INCAR:

TagValueWhy
NSW100 Maximum ionic steps. If you hit this, the relaxation did not finish — it did not fail loudly, it just stopped.
IBRION2 Conjugate gradient. Robust and a good default. (IBRION = 1, quasi-Newton, is faster once you are already near the minimum.)
ISIF3 The important one. 2 relaxes only the ions inside a fixed cell; 3 also relaxes the cell shape and volume. We want 3, because the lattice constant is the thing we are predicting.
EDIFFG−0.01 Negative means "force criterion in eV/Å". Positive would mean an energy criterion, which is a weaker condition and not what you want.
NELMIN4 Minimum SCF steps per ionic step. After atoms move, the first SCF step can look deceptively converged; this forces it to actually re-solve.
ISYM2 Use symmetry to save time. Safe for a perfect crystal. You will turn this off for defects — a defect breaks the symmetry, and keeping ISYM on can prevent the atoms from relaxing into the correct lower-symmetry structure.
Why ISIF = 3 is the one that needs a converged ENCUT. Only ISIF = 3 changes the cell volume, and only a changing volume changes which plane waves fit under the cutoff. That is the Pulay stress mechanism from module 04, Part D. A fixed-cell relaxation (ISIF = 2) is much more forgiving. So the exposure is specific, not general — and it happens to apply to exactly the calculation you are running here.

3. Running it

cd tutorials/05_si_relax
conda activate defects
cp ~/vasp_pseudo/Si/POTCAR POTCAR

# edit INCAR: set ENCUT to your converged value
# edit KPOINTS: set your converged mesh

./run_local.sh

The run_local.sh script is the laptop equivalent of a cluster submit script. It checks the inputs exist, verifies that the POSCAR species count matches the POTCAR, runs VASP, and then reports whether the relaxation actually converged instead of leaving you to assume it.

Read the script. It is short, and the POSCAR/POTCAR consistency check in it is the automated version of the reflex module 03 told you to build. Building checks like this into your tooling is how you stop relying on remembering.

4. Watching it converge

In a second terminal:

tail -f OSZICAR

You will see blocks of DAV: lines (electronic steps) punctuated by F= lines (one per ionic step). What healthy convergence looks like:

  • Each electronic block gets shorter as the relaxation proceeds — later ionic steps start from a better guess.
  • The energy on the F= lines decreases monotonically, and by smaller amounts each time.
  • Silicon from a near-correct starting structure should finish in only a handful of ionic steps.

Warning signs:

  • Energy going up and down between ionic steps. Usually EDIFF too loose, so the forces are noisy.
  • Electronic blocks hitting NELM every time. Try ALGO = Normal if you were on Fast.
  • Many ionic steps with tiny energy changes. Often means EDIFFG is tighter than your electronic noise floor — you are chasing numerical dust.

5. Analyzing the result

python analyze_relax.py

This reports the initial and relaxed lattice constants, the volume change, the space group of the relaxed structure, whether the force criterion was met, and the error against experiment.

Check the space group. Relaxed silicon must still be Fd-3m. If it is not, something moved that should not have — usually a symptom of a bad starting structure or an ISYM problem. A relaxation that quietly lowers the symmetry of a perfect crystal is a bug, not a discovery.

Doing it by hand too

Once, do it without the script, so you know what the script is doing:

# the relaxed structure is CONTCAR, same format as POSCAR
head -5 CONTCAR

# did it converge?
grep "reached required accuracy" OUTCAR

# how many ionic steps?
grep -c "free  energy   TOTEN" OUTCAR

# final energy
grep "free  energy   TOTEN" OUTCAR | tail -1
CONTCAR is the relaxed structure. To continue a relaxation that ran out of steps, cp CONTCAR POSCAR and rerun. This is one of the most useful moves in VASP and you will do it constantly. Keep the original POSCAR somewhere first.

6. Interpreting the error

You should get something close to 5.46 Å, against an experimental 5.431 Å — an error of a few tenths of a percent, on the high side.

That error is not a mistake, and it is not fixable by converging harder. PBE systematically underbinds covalent semiconductors and overestimates their lattice constants by a few tenths of a percent. It is a known, documented, reproducible property of the functional. You converged the numerics in module 04; what remains is the physics of the approximation.

The useful skill is holding both error sources in mind at once and knowing which is which:
  • Numerical error — your fault, fixable, and you should have already eliminated it. Detected by convergence testing.
  • Functional error — a property of the method, not fixable within it, but characterizable. You handle it by knowing its typical size and direction for your class of material, and by comparing against experiment or a better method.
This distinction is about to matter far more than it does here. In module 06 you will find that PBE gets silicon's band gap wrong by roughly a factor of two — a much larger functional error, and one that will dominate the interpretation of every defect level you compute this semester. Meeting the idea now, on a 0.4% lattice constant error, is much easier than meeting it for the first time on a 50% band gap error.
